## Step 2 — Search every available page for comparable dot groups

Once the pilot measurements are validated, run a repeatable detector across the complete available page corpus.

The detector should identify candidate groups with three, five, six, and other numbers of dots, recording raw coordinates and detection parameters. It should retain rejected candidates and distinguish likely decorative marks from runes, text, and scan artifacts.

This answers the key question: are pages 24, 40 and 73 genuinely unusual, or are similar geometric configurations common throughout the book?

## Step 3 — Build the statistical control

Compare the five-dot motif against other five-dot groups and randomly selected five-point subsets from the manuscript.

For each comparison, use the same distance normalization and transformation models. Account for all point permutations and transformation types searched, because finding the best match among thousands of possibilities will naturally produce some very low residuals.

The result should include an empirical null distribution and a multiple-comparison-adjusted significance estimate where the data support one.

## Step 4 — Investigate cipher relevance only if the geometry survives

If the five-dot arrangement remains unusual after controlling for reused artwork and search flexibility, define a specific hypothesis connecting a measurable property to the manuscript.

For example, a point-ordering rule must be defined before extracting a sequence. Its output must make a prediction that can fail, and the same rule must be tested on control motifs.

Do not begin trying arbitrary rune substitutions or assigning symbolic meanings to the points yet.

## What I found
